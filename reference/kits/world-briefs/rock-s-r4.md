## Where it stands, told plainly
Three rounds: 4 and 4.5, then 5 and 5.5, then 5 and 5. It has stopped climbing, so this round changes what the rock is made of, not its places.
What stands and must not be lost: one solid body, the socket with the ball sunk in it, the outline on picture 9, the roof's dark front, no pixel out of the palette, the live page untouched.
What it is, said harshly, at the page's size: A POCKET OF PALE TAN BOULDERS IN A BLACK BOX. Open r9-0-picture-2x.jpg (shots/world/rock-r/) beside s3-final-beside.jpg and look at what the picture's rock is made of. It is not boulders. Every mass is a STACK OF THIN SHARP SHEETS, like slate or shale broken along its bedding: the sheets fan, lean and curl in toward the ball, their ends are pointed and angular, and the dark lines that give the picture its detail are the shadowed edges between one sheet and the next. Brandon's words were that theirs looks more detailed "because of where their cracks are and how the lighting interacts with it": the cracks are the partings between sheets, and they follow each mass's own lean. And the picture is not tan: its brights are gold, its mids are a deep saturated red orange, its darks a red brown. Round 2 was all orange, round 3 all tan. Both are wrong the same way: one color at every brightness.

## Four things, in this order
1. SHEETS, IN THE FORM. Each great mass has a bedding: a direction its sheets lie in, leaning in toward the ball and different for every mass. Cut the mass into sheets along it, 4 to 14 units thick, a thick one beside two thin ones, never evenly. Each sheet ends where it broke: pulled back or standing proud of its neighbours by 2 to 14 units, with an outline that is angular and ragged and comes to points. So a mass's silhouette is a fan of sheet ends and never a boulder's rounded outline, a face seen flat on is the top sheet with the broken ends of the next ones showing beyond it, and a mass seen edge on (the plates left of the ball) is a comb of thin lit lips with black between. Where sheets part, the parting is a dark slot the ball does not reach, 1 to 4 units wide: those are the cracks. No groove drawn across a face, no X, no net. The roof's underside and the great right slab, which are flat panels and a board now, are made the same way.
   You will need a finer mesh for a 4 unit sheet to keep a crisp edge: about 1.2 units to a cell within 2.2 R of the ball, coarser beyond. The budget is raised for it (below).
2. SHEETS, FINER, IN THE STONE. The rock is drawn once and kept as two pictures (src/pieces/world/kept.ts), so what the stone costs per pixel is paid once, not every frame: for sculpt S it may spend freely (the strip under the ball that is drawn live is the exception: give its cost). Store each vertex's bedding direction in the file (the spare attributes: say which in the file's meta) and let the stone lay fine laminae of 0.8 to 3 units along it, as steps in the surface with a lit lip toward the ball and a shadow under it, fading out where they would be finer than a pixel so nothing shimmers. Geometry gives the sheets you can count, the stone gives the ones you cannot. Mend what the reviewers found at three times while you are in it: identical square pit studs, a smear on sloped faces, concentric swirl rings in the noise, smooth clay on the floor's top.
3. THE PICTURE'S COLORS, BY MEASUREMENT AND NOT BY EYE. Write a script that reads picture 9's hollow (the rock only: mask out the ball, the lines of light, the land and the skin) and gives, for each of about 16 steps of brightness, the mean color of the picture's rock at that brightness. That curve is the truth: gold at the top, a saturated red orange through the middle (26 percent of the picture's lit pixels sit at hue 10 to 20 degrees, and S has 2.5: near the ball the mids are 0.42 green over red and 0.10 blue over red, further out 0.53 and 0.25), a red brown at the foot. Then make sculpt S's stone land on it: whatever amount of the ball's light reaches a place, its color is the picture's color for that brightness, with a little difference from mass to mass. Show the curve for the picture, for round 3 and for yours as three strips one over another, and the same table of hue and saturation as before.
4. THE LIGHT AT THE PICTURE'S PLACES. Luma in picture 9's pixels, the picture then round 3. Light: the left lobe (495, 360) 125, 31. The ball's left side (575, 470) 126, 78. The right slabs (820, 380) 135, 72 and (900, 430) 114, 64. The masses hanging at the lower left (330, 570) 39, 4. Darken: the back wall's wedge (545, 470) 14, 68. The floor's top (650, 532) 51, 99, and beside the stream (600, 520) 36, 105 and (760, 520) 29, 132. The rings: within 1.3 R, 22 percent lit in the picture and 53 in S. From 1.3 to 1.8 R, 15 lit and 25 dark in the picture, 10 and 38 in S (round 2 had this ring right: do not lose it again). From 1.8 to 3.5 R, 9 to 10 percent lit rims in the picture and 1 to 3 in S, and the darks out there are 12 to 24 in the picture and 0 to 6 in S: a brown dark, not a black box. If the inner ring shades the outer masses, open it where the picture has light passing (look at ?stonedebug=2).
