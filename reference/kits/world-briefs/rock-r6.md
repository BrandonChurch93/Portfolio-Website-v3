## Why one builder
Until now one builder made the sculpt and another its stone, and each round both reports said the same thing: the stone needs to know which slab a place is on and how near a crease it is, and the sculpt needs the stone to give its edges a lip. Both scored 4 this round with the same words from four reviewers: "torn paper", "cut card", "birch bark". You have both. Store with every vertex whatever the stone needs (a slab number, a signed distance to the nearest crease, which way the slab's layers run) and use it.

## What must change, most important first
1. THICKNESS. Every slab reads as a sheet of torn paper with a drop shadow, all in about one plane. A slab is a slab: seen from the fixed view it shows a lit face toward the core AND a dark side face several units deep along its torn edge, it overhangs toward the visitor, and it throws black onto the slab behind it, as picture 9's roof and left slabs do. Where two meet there is a warm dark in the corner (the crease distance you store), and across a face turned from the core the bounce falls off, so no face is one flat tone to a hard edge.
2. THE STONE'S MARKS ARE HATCHING. Short black strokes of one size and one spacing on every lit face: "birch bark", "pencil hatching", "a repeating stamp". Bedding is long, shallow, real: grooves that run through a whole slab along its layers, lit on one lip and shadowed on the other, of very different lengths and depths, on some slabs and not on others (the slab number you store). Under the core they must not radiate as a sunburst.
3. THE BOWL is a uniform pale peach disc ((221, 143, 76) falling only to (196, 125, 69) across 80 picture pixels: the light falls 1.3 times from the ball to its lip, and the right ring is a third of a stop over). Smaller, a deeper orange (the picture's blue channel there is about 40, not 75), with ledge steps in it and hot orange in every recess, and more of the ring made of slab ends at different angles so lit and dark faces alternate.
4. THE ROOF AND THE FLOOR. The roof is a thin board over a flat black wall: build the picture's thick crust, a pale grey brown broken band from 268 to about 335 down with chunky lit top edges, and its two hanging lobes to about 400 down at 450 to 520 and 830 to 900 across. It is the SAME stone, greyed by the moon, with its grain running on (it reads as a second material, a grey pumice). The floor is a lit lid ending on black air: a thick ledge, its lit top at 535 to 550, a dark undercut face to 575 to 600, and dim broken rock hanging under each side to about 700 down at 330 across and 690 at 1040 (under the floor is 87 percent black where the picture's is 5).
5. FAULTS. The dead straight vertical seam at picture 688 across (the world's x 0) from the lip down to about 335, and the flat box wall to its right. The staircase of square teeth at about 565 to 575 across, 440 to 460 down. Small sawtooth combs on the cell at the top of the bowl and along the right slab's long edge. Soft edgeless dark smudges on the cave's faces.
6. THREE TIMES. Lit faces are as smooth as clay where the light is strongest: grain and pitting seen there, hue that rolls with the light (deep orange in recesses, cream only where a face squarely meets the core: 61 percent of the lit stone sits in one hue bin where the picture's spreads across three), darks a neutral brown and not rust.
